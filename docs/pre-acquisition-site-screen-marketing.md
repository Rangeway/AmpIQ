# Pre-Acquisition Site Screen Marketing Kit

## LinkedIn post

### A design fee credited toward construction is not always a discount.

A property owner asks whether a site can support EV charging. The common answer is a design-build proposal: pay for early engineering now, and the fee is credited if the same firm builds the project.

That can be a sensible path for a simple, well-understood site. It is not automatically the lowest-cost or lowest-risk path.

The question is not whether the design fee is credited. The question is what the owner has bought by accepting it.

Before treating a credit as savings, ask four things:

1. Who owns the work product?
2. Can the design be used to competitively bid installation?
3. Has the scope been right-sized for the property, or simply optimized for one firm’s preferred solution?
4. Can the owner see the credit alongside the value of keeping competitive tension in the construction bid?

Independent diligence costs money, but it can preserve choice at the point where the money gets real. A desk-based screen can tell an owner whether a site even deserves that next level of work. A separate technical scope can then make the construction market compete for the owner’s business.

The right answer is site-specific. The point is to make the tradeoff visible before the decision is made for you.

## Video series: “Should I buy this site for EV charging?”

### Video 1: The question before the project

**Opening:** “The most expensive EV charging decision is often made before anyone asks whether the site can work.”

**Core:** Explain that a property buyer does not need an engineered design to ask the first question. They need a decision-quality first screen: utility context, parcel realities, local constraints, and the specific unknowns that require a real site visit.

**Close:** “Before you commit to a site, make sure it deserves the next dollar of diligence.”

### Video 2: “The utility question” is not one question

**Opening:** “Can the utility support charging here? That sounds like one question. It is usually four.”

**Core:** Explain the difference between identifying the utility territory, observing public clues, initiating a utility conversation, and receiving capacity or upgrade confirmation. Make clear that only the last is a utility answer.

**Close:** “A good first screen tells you which of those questions matters for this parcel.”

### Video 3: A construction credit is not automatically savings

**Opening:** “If a contractor credits engineering toward the build, it may be a discount. It may also be the moment competitive tension disappears.”

**Core:** Use the four ownership, bid, right-sizing, and transparency questions from the LinkedIn post. State that a design-build path can be right for some sites, but the owner should understand the tradeoff.

**Close:** “The best early work preserves your ability to make a better decision later.”

### Video 4: Model without incentives

**Opening:** “Incentives can improve a charging project. They should not be the reason a weak project becomes a yes.”

**Core:** Explain that program availability, eligibility, funding, and timing change. First determine whether the site works on its own economics and operational case. Then treat qualifying incentives as potential upside, verified before reliance.

**Close:** “Build the decision around the property. Let incentives improve it, not rescue it.”

## Suggested production format

- 45 to 75 seconds each.
- Direct-to-camera delivery, with a single on-screen question at the opening.
- Use one simplified site plan, utility map, or decision-memo excerpt as B-roll. Do not show client information.
- End every video with: “Considering a property for EV charging? Start with the site screen.”
